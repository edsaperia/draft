🪶 docs.vote

The approval threshold

How docs.vote decides whether a proposal passes — a change to the text, or a change to one of the rules.

The question the card asks

🌡️ asks how sure the members have to be that a proposal is better than what stands, at the close, where a decision is permanent. The same number governs a change to a paragraph and a change to a rule: whatever a proposal is about, it passes the same way.

Sure of what, exactly? Not of a share of the votes. The number is how certain the document can be, from the votes cast so far, that the proposal really is the better one. A vote on docs.vote is a choice between two things — this proposal, or what it would replace — and the document adds those choices up into a single probability that the proposal wins.

Almost nobody needs to think about this. The card asks for one number and the document does the rest. This page is for the people who want to know what the number is doing, and why it is not the vote threshold they are used to.

Why not a vote threshold with a quorum?

The familiar rule is two thirds in favour, with at least half the membership voting. It is a fine rule for a meeting where everybody is in the room at once and one question is on the table. docs.vote is neither, and the approval threshold does better on every count below.

It waits for evidence rather than counting heads

Under a vote threshold, three votes for and none against passes the moment the quorum is met. But three people agreeing is not proof that a wording is better; a document that treated it as proof would replace its own text on thin evidence and spend the rest of its life changing its mind. Here the proposal stays open and the votes keep arriving until there are enough of them to be sure. That is why the share needed falls as votes come in — the rule has not moved, the evidence has.

Nobody has to be present at once

A vote threshold with a quorum needs a moment when the ballot is called and counted. On docs.vote votes arrive as people arrive, over hours or days, and every vote moves the answer a little; a proposal passes the moment the document is sure enough, and not before. Nothing has to be called. (There is still a quorum on docs.vote — 👥 — but it asks a different question: whether enough of the membership is still here for the document to go on at all. It is about the membership being present, never about how many votes one proposal has.)

An absent member is not a vote against

With a quorum, the people who did not turn up decide the outcome: too few and nothing can pass, however lopsided the votes. Here only votes cast are counted, and the number of them is the evidence. A member who has not voted has said nothing, and is treated as having said nothing.

A share of what?

A vote threshold assumes one question with a yes and a no. Here several rival wordings can be in one race, votes are between two at a time, and two members may be shown different pairs — so there is no votes for this one to take a share of: a proposal may have beaten one rival and lost to another. How sure are we that this beats what stands is defined whatever the field looks like, and it is the thing the document actually has to know before it replaces anything.

One number, whatever the membership

80% is near-unanimity in a membership of five and a comfortable majority in one of fifty. One setting serves both, and it goes on meaning the same thing as the membership changes. A share of the votes and a quorum would have to be chosen again for each size of membership, and chosen again every time somebody joined or left.

It is honest about small memberships

A vote threshold lets four people carry a rule by three to one and call it a two-thirds majority. Here four votes cannot make the document 95% sure however they fall, so the proposal waits — and 🌡️ itself says the highest number the membership as it stands can reach, so nobody sets a threshold that nothing could ever clear.

A vote of no preference counts

A member who prefers neither wording says so, and that is evidence too: it says the two are about the same, which pulls toward waiting rather than toward either one. A ballot has no way to say that; an abstention there is simply a missing vote.

And it rises toward the close

An early change can still be challenged; a late one is what the document ends up saying. So where the founder chose a rising threshold it starts lower when voting opens — starting at 55% when voting opens is the clause's own wording — and climbs to the number 🌡️ asks for. A vote threshold has no natural way to do this.

Every number on this page is the number at the close, which is the highest it gets. Earlier in the document's life a proposal needs fewer votes than the table says.
