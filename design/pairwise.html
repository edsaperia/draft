🪶 docs.vote

The approval threshold

How a document decides that a change is better than the words it replaces.

The question the card asks

🌡️ asks how sure the members have to be that a change is better than the words it replaces, at the close, where an adoption is permanent.

Sure of what, exactly? Not of a share of the votes. The number is how confident the document can be, from the votes cast so far, that the new wording really is the better one. A vote on docs.vote is a choice between two wordings — this one, or the one it would replace — and the document adds those choices up into a single probability that the change wins.

Almost nobody needs to think about this. The card asks for one number and the document does the rest. This page is for the people who want to know what the number is doing.

What one number means in votes

The same number is a different count of people in different rooms, and a different count again as more votes arrive. Below, every whole percent from 55 to 95, against the votes cast on one change — and every number here is the number at the close.

    Why it is done this way

    Few votes, and it waits

    Three votes for and none against is not proof that a wording is better; it is three people agreeing. A document that treated it as proof would replace its own text on thin evidence and spend the rest of its life changing its mind. This one waits instead: the change stays in the race, and the votes keep arriving, until there are enough of them to be sure. That is why the share needed falls as votes come in — the rule has not moved, the evidence has.

    A share of what?

    Votes here are between two wordings at a time. Where three wordings are in one race there is no votes for this one to take a share of: it may have beaten one rival and lost to another, and two members may have been shown different pairs. How sure are we that this beats the current text is defined whatever the field looks like, and it is the thing the document actually has to know before it replaces anything.

    One number, every room

    80% is near-unanimity in a cohort of five and a comfortable majority in a convention of fifty. One setting serves both, and it goes on meaning the same thing as the membership grows. A share of the votes would have to be chosen again for every room, and chosen again every time somebody joined.

    And it rises toward the close

    An early change can still be challenged; a late one is what the document ends up saying. So where the founder chose a rising threshold it starts lower when voting opens — starting at 55% when voting opens is the clause's own wording — and climbs to the number 🌡️ asks for.

    Every number on this page is the number at the close, which is the highest it gets. Earlier in the document's life a change needs fewer votes than the table says.

    Votes of no preference

    A member who prefers neither wording says so, and that is evidence too: it says the two are about the same, which pulls toward waiting rather than toward either one. The chart and the table above count votes for and against only.
