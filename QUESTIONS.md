1394. **The first accessibility audit: the instrument, and the three plain defects.** (Raised 2026-09-16 from an overnight pass. 1395. **The glyph is the name, and a judgment's two options share one.** (Raised 2026-09-16, same pass.) Two halves of one question — *what does a member hear where the surface shows a picture*.

    **(a) The lanes, and this is the audit's worst row: 79 sightings on 27 cards**, every judgment card the charter serves. A decision card's three `.lanepick` buttons carry **no `role`** and sit in **no `radiogroup`** (there is not one on the charter; the eighteen in `band.js` and `setup.js` are the founder's setting cards). A screen reader reads `"Prefer this Preferred"`, `"Prefer this Preferred"`, `"Indifferent Indifferent"` — so **the two rival wordings have the same name**, nothing says *one of three* or which is chosen, and the name carries the resting *and* selected labels at once, saying an option is preferred whether or not it is. This is CP1 working exactly as designed — *the option's name lives on the block, not the button*, already a named gotcha for breaking `journey`'s label helpers — seen from the side where the block is not read at all. **The fix is not to rename the buttons**: it is `aria-labelledby` pointing at the `.opttext`, a `radiogroup` round the lane bar, `role="radio"` with `aria-checked`, and the selected word out of the name. Markup, not copy; moves a frozen reference.

    **(b) The glyph-only names**, 76 sightings for the discard 🗑️ alone: a reader hears *wastebasket* for discard and withdraw, *pencil* for propose, *snowflake* for chill, *memo* for the edit door, *black nib* and *shield* for the founder's powers, *classical building* for 🏛️, *black right-pointing small triangle* for the fold — and on a phone the task drawer's name is the bare count, **"8"**. The vocabulary is glyphic by ruling and that is not in question. What is: **an `aria-label` is a string a member reads**, so by this project's own rule it lives in `design/copy.js` and passes STYLE.md's T1–T49 — which makes this a copy decision with a copy owner, and the largest body of member-readable text the surface has never written. `a#quill` reads `🪶🪶🪶🪶` at the birth, the wallet's count bleeding into the link.

1396. **Nothing on the page announces itself, under a 4 s poll.** (Raised 2026-09-16, same pass.) There is **no `aria-live`, `role="status"`, `role="alert"` or `role="log"` anywhere on the surface** — zero across all four epochs. This bites harder here than on most pages because arriving-without-being-asked-for *is* the product: `remoteCS` re-hydrates on a 4 s poll, the rail is rebuilt wholesale on every render, and the `needs-you-queue`'s whole design is that a task appears beside its clause. 1397. **What the keyboard cannot reach, and where it is left standing.** (Raised 2026-09-16, same pass.)

    **(0) Every act drops the keyboard at the top of the page — driven, and the same answer six times out of six.** Enter on a clause tab opens its card and focus falls to `<body>` (3 of 3); Enter on an enabled ✓ commits the judgment and focus falls to `<body>` (3 of 3). A decision card replaces its own paragraph when it opens and runs its whole box back onto that paragraph when it closes — the two motions the surface is proudest of — and both destroy the element the keyboard was standing on, with nothing catching it. What it costs is the product: after one judgment a member is back at the top with 189 tab stops between them and the next question, so casting ten votes is not credible. **The act itself works** — the ✓ is properly focusable and Enter commits it, in both gesture positions — so this is the aftermath only, and the fix is to hand focus to the entry the card collapsed into, or to the clause's tab, at both ends. SURFACE C1–C5 and L1–L9 govern what opens and closes a card and what it focuses; whether *what it focuses* has ever meant the keyboard is the question.

    Then three groups of reachability, and only the first is clearly a defect. **(a) The wallets are not focusable** — `#wallet`, `#penwallet`, `#shieldwallet`, `#voicewallet` carry no `tabindex` and are not natively focusable, on every epoch including the birth; they are controls, a hold starts on them, and the keyboard cannot start one. **(b) The tabs behind the front of a stack are `aria-hidden`** (143 sightings): `design/session.js:2969` reads `(behind ? ' aria-hidden="true"' : ' role="button" tabindex="0"')`, so in a `tab-stack` only the front tab is in the accessibility tree, though each behind it is a *different decision* on the same clause. This may well be right — the rail carries an entry for every one (M1–M6), so they are unreachable only *from the document* — and is named because the reasoning is written down nowhere. **(c) A decided tab is in the focus order with no role**: `:3104` and `:3163` emit `tabindex="0"` without `role="button"` where `:2969`, twelve lines away, emits both; axe's `focus-order-semantics`, 26 nodes, the one row axe found that no hand probe did. **Checked and cleared:** `disabled` controls are correctly unfocusable (the ✓ greyed until something is chosen, and a closed document taking no edit) — 88 sightings of correct behaviour, and a focus ring *is* drawn, eight `:focus-visible` rules including `.achip:focus-visible`, which is itself the evidence that a clause tab is meant to be reachable. **The hold position, reached by `--gesture=hold`:** the ✓ is still focusable and Enter still commits it, `submit` going through the click path in both positions. But **`draft-propose` returns early under hold** (`design/session.js:3520`, *held, not clicked*) and the hold is driven by document-level `pointerdown`/`pointerup` (`:2823`, `:2839`) with no `keydown` bound for it anywhere — the surface's only keydown handlers are the lane chooser's Enter/Space (`:3499`), edit-mode's and the wallet's Escape. So under the hold gesture a keyboard user can **choose** an option and cannot **spend** one. Not driven to proof, because the fixture has no enabled `draft-propose` on any of its 48 cards. **SURFACE §7.2's switch is an accessibility switch as well as a design one**, which is worth knowing before the trial (backlog 184) is flipped.

925. **Three more things git knows about overlapping patches, parked** (raised 2026-08-27 with 924; **Ed: park for now** — each wants design work on how it is communicated before it is worth a mechanism). **(a) Dependent candidates.** `propose-edit` (K24) starts a draft from a candidate's wording, so C can be built on unadopted A; if A loses, C's rebase onto the incumbent fails over a decision made elsewhere. *Presupposes* is a different relation from *composes* (§2.2's lattice), and git's stacked patches say: declare it at submission, adopt in order, and let C fall with A or return to its author with A's fate stated. Verify what the engine does today first. **(b) A candidate's version chain.** §2.4's blanket *evidence resets* does not distinguish a clean rebase (no words changed) from an author's revision; Mercurial's obsolescence markers give a change an identity across rewrites, each recording what it superseded and why, so the rule can be *rebase keeps, revision resets*. With it, git's `rerere`: an author's confirmation against new text is remembered and reused when the same span moves again, instead of the leader failing identically every batch. **(c) Two cheap ones**, no spec change: a base-independent content hash of the normalised hunk (`patch-id`) before the dedup gate asks the LLM, and an interdiff — what moved under you, not the whole new paragraph — on the confirm-against-new-text card.

282. **A rendered document and a source offset do not agree** (raised 2026-08-17, from 279's build; Ed asked for a recommendation the same day). The charter fixture is plain text, so the composer can take a caret offset straight from the DOM and use it against the clause's source. The moment the *document* renders markdown that stops being true: a DOM selection measures the rendered text, and `**bold**` is four characters there and eight in the source, so every offset the composer computes is wrong by two for each mark above the caret.

**Recommendation: render markdown in the document, and make the rendered→source mapping a property of the block rather than of the composer.** One function that takes a block element and a DOM position and returns a *source* offset, used by everything that asks the question — `offsetIn`, `startDraftFromTyping`, `startDraftFromRun`. The arithmetic already exists (`richToSource` / `sourceToRich`, written for the lane's view toggle); what matters is that exactly one place knows about the distinction, because the failure mode here is a two-character drift that nothing complains about and no test catches unless the test happens to put a mark above the caret.

Two other options, for the record. Keep the charter plain and let markdown live only inside candidates — cheapest, but an adopted candidate would then print its asterisks into the charter, so it only defers the problem to the first adoption. Or store the document rendered and treat markdown purely as import/export — which gives up "the characters you see are what is stored", and that was the whole reason 279 chose source-of-truth markdown in the first place.

**Do it before the charter carries any formatting**, not after. Until then every test passes and the bug does not exist; the day somebody bolds a word near the top of a long clause it exists everywhere at once, and it will look like a caret bug rather than an offset bug.

**Deferred past the alpha, deliberately** (Ed, 2026-08-27, answering this from the alpha-readiness plan's Track 3.3 — plan-queue **77**, backlog entry 77; the plan had said *fix before the day*). Option **(b)**: the charter stays plain text for the alpha and the rendering is done afterwards. Nothing changes before the day.

Three facts the deferral rests on, checked in the tree at `0d160ad` rather than assumed. **The charter renders plain text today** — `design/session.js:2994` and `:3009` emit `esc(line.x)`, so a clause carrying `**bold**` prints its asterisks — which means the bug is not merely unobserved but *unreachable*: `offsetIn` (`design/session.js:1875`) measures rendered text, and for a plain-text document that **is** the source. **The recommendation above is not a bug fix**, it is *render markdown in the document*, a visible change to what every reader of every charter sees; the arithmetic it needs already exists as `richToSource` / `sourceToRich` (`design/cards.js:342`, `:352`), but the change also moves `design/tools/card-copy.golden.json`, both frozen probes, `scripts/journey-walk.mjs`'s caret and propose steps, and what `design/STYLE.md` takes a clause to be. And **the alpha is short**: reaching the bug needs somebody to bold a word *and* that proposal to win, where the measured preset produces about one adoption per fifteen-minute session (`PRODUCTION.md`'s running log, 2026-08-27).

What the deferral costs, stated so nobody rediscovers it as a surprise: **if an alpha room does adopt a clause carrying a mark, that document's offsets are quietly wrong from then on** — in a throwaway document, which is the whole of why this is affordable. The two other options are on the record above and neither is rejected; (c), building only the block-to-source mapper without changing what renders, remains the cheap half of (a) if the rendering is wanted in a hurry.

What it needs a ruling on is the evidence. A revision is a **new candidate**: any comparisons already cast against the old wording were cast against a text that no longer exists, so they are discarded exactly as a ground shift discards yours (§4.4). That is correct and it is also a real cost — revise a proposal that six people have already judged and you have thrown their work away, and they will be served the pair again. Three ways. (a) **Always allowed, evidence always resets** — simple, honest, and quietly expensive on a busy race. (b) **A grace window**: free revision until the first judgment lands on it, and after that it is withdraw-and-repropose with the cost visible. This matches what the request is actually about — *immediately*, because they saw a mistake — and it makes the common case free and the expensive case explicit. (c) Revision only as **surgery on the wording**, with evidence carried over where the change is below some edit-distance floor; cheapest for the room and the hardest to explain, since it makes the ranking depend on a diff threshold. Recommendation: **(b)**, with the card saying which side of the window it is on.

314. **Seeing which card other members have open** (Ed, 2026-08-17, and **shelved by him in the same breath** — *let's shelve this for now until we actually test with a group of real humans, otherwise this is quite speculative*). 346. **Sybil resistance at the front door** (Ed, 2026-08-18, parked: *if people can become members via a link, we have to be wary of sybil attacks — perhaps even accidental*). *Note, 2026-08-26 (entry 94): the open door is now 🤝 *yes* with 🪪 at ✒️ — an application admitted on submit — which is exactly where this bites; the same price at 🏛️ or ✏️ puts a room between the link and the membership.* Membership begins at first arrival (§9.6a) and the magic link **is** the credential, so the attack surface is the invitation itself. Ed’s own example is the subtlest and the most likely: **one person accidentally becoming two members on two devices** — possible only if membership is minted per *arrival* rather than per *invitation*, so the structural rule is that however many devices open a link, they land in one membership: a link must never create an identity, only unlock one. Three more vectors, one of them innocent: a **forwarded link** logs the bearer in *as the invitee* — two people quietly sharing one membership, or a colleague “helpfully” opening a link that was not theirs; a **double invitation** (one person at two addresses) makes one person two members, two wallets, two ceremony answers and two places in quorum; and deliberate padding by whoever controls invitations. Sharpened by §9.6a itself: a duplicate discovered **after the start** cannot be removed, because removal is a constitutional motion needing every member’s consent including the duplicate’s — so whatever the defence is, it has to work **before** judging opens. Candidate defences for later, cheapest first: (a) first-open **confirmation** (“you are Ida Fenwick — is that you?”), with later opens of the same link joining the same membership rather than a new one — note the tension with single-use links, which would lock the person’s second device out instead; (b) the convenor’s pre-start window is the human check — arrivals are visible, uninviting is free, and the start is the deadline for noticing; (c) a **merge** operation for discovered duplicates that is not an expulsion (one person’s two memberships becoming one re-rates nothing they did not do themselves) — needs care where both halves answered the ceremony differently. Deliberate sybil by an adversarial *convenor* is out of scope: they control the roster before the start anyway, and the defence against a bad-faith convenor is not joining their document.

315. **The ranking model as a document-creation setting: approval · Bradley–Terry · Crowd-BT** (Ed, 2026-08-17, parked as a note for later). *There are situations where each is appropriate and I think much of our machinery still applies for each.* Crowd-BT is Chen, Bennett, Collins-Thompson & Horvitz, *Pairwise Ranking Aggregation in a Crowdsourced Setting*, WSDM 2013 (10.1145/2433396.2433420) — Bradley–Terry with a **per-judge reliability parameter** estimated jointly with the item scores, plus an active-learning scheme that chooses which pair to serve *and whom to serve it to*. Details want checking against the paper before anything is built on them.

Ed is right that most of the machinery is indifferent to the choice. The `patch-engine`, `overlap-gates` and `dedup-gate` are text machinery and never touch the model. The `adoption-threshold` and its ramp are stated as P(challenger beats incumbent), and all three produce something that plays that role. Quorum, adoption floors, the record, the gazette, and the whole of `session-view` are unaffected — a card asks for a judgment either way.

What genuinely changes is worth listing, because it is more than the estimator. **Active sampling largely disappears under approval**: everybody can rate everything, so the `router` stops *selecting* and only *orders*, which would make the queue dramatically shorter and the `evidence-meter` mean something different. **Deadlock (§8.3) is a Bradley–Terry construct** — *close relative to the marginal value of further sampling* — and under approval it becomes two candidates with similar approval rates, which is detectable but is not the same claim. **Indifference** has no separate existence under approval; approving both *is* indifference. And the **`bounty score`** rests on resolvable disagreement, which is model-specific.

Two things worth having written down before this is picked up.

**The choice is constitutional, not statistical.** Crowd-BT's reliability parameter says a judge is *often wrong*, which presupposes there is a right answer to be wrong about. This project's §3.3 says the opposite in as many words — *preference is not measurement*. If Hollis prefers the shorter clause and thirteen people prefer the longer one, Hollis is not unreliable; Hollis disagrees. Down-weighting a judge is **disenfranchising them by an algorithm nobody in the room can audit**, which is a serious thing to do to a member of a constitutional convention. But there are documents where it is exactly right — a technical standard, a specification being cleaned up, any domain where some readings are simply mistakes — and those are the same documents where a large roster contains people of very different expertise. So the real question a convenor is answering is **does this document treat disagreement as difference or as error**, which is the kind of question that belongs at `document-creation` or the `founding-ceremony` beside quorum and the bar, and not in a settings menu. Under the §9.0a logic it is arguably the roster's to settle rather than the convenor's: being told your judgments count for less is a thing done *to* you.

**And the sophisticated model is probably wrong at v1 scale.** Crowd-BT spends data on extra per-judge parameters, and CLAUDE.md tunes v1 for rosters of 5–10 where data-efficiency beats throughput; on fourteen judges it may be strictly worse than plain Bradley–Terry, which is itself chosen over approval *because* it extracts more per judgment. Crowd-BT's home is hundreds of annotators. Which puts its threshold at roughly the roster size where you stop being able to know everyone — the same point at which a room stops being able to vouch for each other's good faith. Those two thresholds coinciding is not a coincidence, and it is the strongest argument that this is a real setting rather than a knob: **the model should change when the room changes kind.**

One reversal to expect if approval is built: it produces the **more legible record**. The `sealed record`'s best sentence — *these two beat what we had and those three did not* — is currently a Bradley–Terry construction in which every number is a probability against the incumbent. Approval counts are less informative and far easier for a lay reader to trust. The more sophisticated model makes the harder-to-explain record, which matters for a document whose legitimacy rests on people believing the record.
